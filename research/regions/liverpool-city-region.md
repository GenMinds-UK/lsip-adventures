## Area summary and key stats

- LSIP covers Halton (inc. Widnes), Knowsley, Liverpool, Sefton, St Helens and Wirral (p.7). ERB is Liverpool Chamber of Commerce, co-owned with Liverpool City Region Combined Authority, supported by Halton, Knowsley and Wirral chambers (p.7).
- Engagement: 51,271 reached electronically (93% SME, 89% non-Chamber members), 1,618 active engagements, 635 (also cited as 634) deep-dive interviews since September 2025 (p.8; Annex C p.5). Deep dives: 93% SME, 83% non-Chamber members; by size 43% small, 27% micro, 23% medium, 7% large (Annex C p.9); by local authority Liverpool 38%, Wirral 14%, Halton 16%, Sefton 12%, St Helens 10%, Knowsley 10% (Annex C p.10).
- Productivity: output per hour is around 11% below the England average; GVA per resident is £27,300, 76% of the UK level (p.16). The 2025 progress report separately cites GVA per person as £25,000, 73% of the UK level (progress report p.8) — both figures appear in the evidence base and are not reconciled in the documents; treated as a genuine discrepancy between sources rather than an error on our part.
- Over 200,000 working-age residents are economically inactive; inactivity is higher among people aged 50–64, women, and linked to long-term sickness and caring responsibilities (p.16). The 2025 progress report gives an inactivity rate of 25% versus ~21% nationally, an employment rate of 71% (LCR ranked 35th of 36 LSIP areas), median pay of £26,362 versus £28,000 nationally (a 6% gap), and 24.2% of the workforce lacking essential employability skills (progress report p.8).
- Business base: around 43,000 active enterprises, ~90% micro/small (p.38). ONS Business Counts: ~89% under 10 employees, ~9% 10–49, ~1–2% medium, ~1% large; SMEs are over 99% of the population (Annex C p.9). Liverpool holds ~35% of enterprises, Wirral ~18%, Sefton ~15%, St Helens ~11%, Knowsley ~9%, Halton ~9% (Annex C p.10).
- AI/digital sector: over 3,000 businesses, ~24,000 jobs, growth outpacing national trends; assets include Sci-Tech Daresbury, the STFC Hartree Centre, the Hartree National Centre for Digital Innovation and the LCR Connect full-fibre network (p.17).

## Priorities

### Cross-cutting: AI and Digital Transformation (pp.17, 23–24)
Positioned as a system-wide requirement, not a sector. Virtually all deep-dive interviews raised digital/AI capability as a critical, immediate gap (p.23). SMEs dominate the business base but lack capacity to assess, adopt and scale AI (p.23). New entrants have theoretical knowledge but lack confidence "using tools in workplace settings," and gaps exist in "ethical and responsible AI use" (p.23, quoted). Training provision is often "too generic, too advanced, or not aligned to real business use cases" (p.23, quoted). Response: an SME-focused AI diagnostic pilot led by **Edge Hill University**, launching Q3 2026 (p.39; Annex B p.2–3), plus a consistent AI capability framework embedded in Level 3–6 provision, linked to the Combined Authority's "AI for Good" and AI Task Force (p.39).

### Priority 1: Professional, Business & Financial Services (PBS) (pp.17–18, 24–25)
~12,000 businesses, ~96,000 employees, ~17,000 students on PBS-related courses/year (p.17). Key stats: distinctive strengths in wealth management, growing maritime legal services linked to the Freeport (p.18). Occupations named: chartered/certified accountants, solicitors, management consultants, legal associate professionals, data analysts, bookkeepers/payroll (Annex A p.6). Verbatim gap quotes:
- "The biggest gaps are in leadership and management capability … and having stronger succession planning in place." — Local Authority, deep dive (p.24)
- "I am not short of motivation to train people, but I simply do not have the time… finding the right course that is actually relevant to the job can be harder than paying for it." — Finance SME, deep dive (p.25)
- "We need people who are genuinely confident using tools like Word and Excel… and able to question and cross-check information." — Small Accountancy Employer, deep dive (p.25)

### Priority 2: Advanced Manufacturing and Clean Energy (pp.18, 26–27)
Over 50,000 manufacturing jobs, £2.9bn annual contribution, 15% of UK automotive output (p.18). Freeport targeting 10,000 jobs; Glass Futures leading industrial decarbonisation; HyNet carbon capture; proposed Mersey Tidal project (p.18). Occupations named: manufacturing operatives, engineering/maintenance technicians, automation/robotics technicians, CNC operators, welders, toolmakers, mechatronics technicians, low-carbon manufacturing technicians (Annex A pp.8–9). Verbatim gap quotes:
- "This will particularly affect welders… who will need stronger skills in programming, operating and troubleshooting robotic systems." — Small Manufacturing Employer, deep dive (p.26)

### Priority 3: Construction and the Built Environment (pp.18–19, 27–29)
32,000+ homes delivered since 2017; retrofit programme improving 10,000 properties; ~2,940 new workers needed to deliver Net Zero for pre-1919 buildings, per Historic England (p.18). 31% of LCR domestic buildings are traditionally constructed vs 20% nationally (p.28). Employer body named: Electrical Contractors' Association (ECA) — reports a widening electrotechnical skills gap and low/very low learner numbers on electrotechnical courses in parts of LCR (p.28). Women in Construction initiative referenced (employer example: Frank Rogers) (p.28, 34). Verbatim gap quotes:
- "They know how to do the work… but haven't had training in organising work or managing people." — Medium Construction Employer, deep dive (p.27)
- "Too many college leavers have never experienced how a construction site operates… health and safety is critical." — Small Cladding Employer, deep dive (p.27)
- "Standards such as ISO 9001 and ISO 14001 are becoming more important." — Construction SME, deep dive (p.28)

### Priority 4: Health, Life Science and Care (pp.19, 29–31)
~7,000 employed in life sciences alone; Life Sciences Innovation Zone up to £800m investment, 8,000 jobs; assets: iiCON, The Pandemic Institute, the Civic Data Cooperative (p.19). Occupations named: care workers/senior care workers, healthcare assistants, registered nurses, registered managers, clinical/diagnostic technologists, laboratory/biomanufacturing technicians, digital health analysts, assistive technology/telecare specialists (Annex A p.13). Verbatim gap quotes:
- "There is a strong need to develop leaders within the NHS who can drive teams forward and support staff to grow." — NHS Large Employer, deep dive (p.29)
- "We particularly struggle to find staff who can drive and have their own vehicle… geography and travel are a big part of the job." — Medium Care Provider, deep dive (p.29)
- "Our biggest upskilling need is basic digital confidence for front-line staff… using mobile apps to log visits and care notes." — Medium Care Provider, deep dive (p.30)

### Priority 5: Visitor Economy (pp.20, 31–32)
50,000+ jobs, ~12% of GVA; Destination Management Plan 2025–2030, coordinated by the government-designated Liverpool City Region Destination Partnership Board (p.20). Roles named: front-line hospitality/attraction staff, visitor experience/events staff, supervisors, SME managers/proprietors, airport ground operations roles linked to Liverpool John Lennon Airport (Annex A p.15). Verbatim gap quotes:
- "Reliability and commitment are the biggest challenges… rather than simply the volume of applicants." — Medium Hotel Employer, deep dive (p.31)
- "The biggest skills gap… is leadership and management capability." — Medium Hotel Employer, deep dive (p.31)
- "AI is increasingly part of the conversation… managers will need to understand tools like Copilot or ChatGPT." — Hotel Business Medium-Sized Employer, deep dive (p.32)
- "Regulation and customer expectations are both increasing… staff must manage stricter food safety rules and complex dietary requirements." — Restaurant SME, deep dive (p.32)

### Priority 6: Creative Industries (pp.19–20, 33–34)
15,000+ employed, £1.2bn contribution; 48% local sector growth vs 29% nationally; clusters in the city centre and Baltic Triangle; Littlewoods Studios investment (p.19). Occupations named: digital content creators/producers, games/immersive developers, creative technologists/virtual production specialists, marketing/digital comms professionals, freelance creative practitioners, mid-career "fusion" roles, technical production/live event specialists, creative business leaders/studio founders (Annex A pp.17–18). Verbatim gap quotes:
- "The big gap is using AI tools properly … and integrating AI into the workflow." — Micro Production Business, deep dive (p.33)

## Cross-cutting themes

1. **AI and Digital Transformation** — see Priority section above; Change 3 in the plan (p.38–39).
2. **Net Zero and Green Skills** — retrofit, low-carbon technologies, environmental compliance needed across construction (esp. heritage buildings), manufacturing and energy, extending into professional services' regulatory/reporting requirements (p.34, 43).
3. **Equality of Opportunity and Inclusive Growth** — NEET young people, women in construction, disadvantaged groups with lower prior attainment; literacy skills pilot; skills passports/wallets/digital badges built into Be More (p.34–35, 42–43).
4. **Progression for the existing workforce, not just entry** (Change 5) — a bottleneck common to every priority sector at Levels 3–6 (p.40–41).
5. **Inclusive pathways and work readiness of new entrants** (Change 6) — literacy, communication and "job-ready" standards; new T Levels, V Levels and Level 2 occupational routes from 2027 (p.42–43).
6. **SME access to workforce development** (Change 2) — ~90% of the ~43,000 active enterprises are micro/small and struggle with time, cost and navigating provision (p.38).
7. **Strategic delivery framework / the "Local Triple Lock"** (Change 1) — LCR Growth Plan (economic direction), Get LCR Working Plan (participation) and the LSIP (skills), delivered through new Industry Skills Partnerships (ISPs), successors to the previous Industry Learning Partnerships (ILPs) (p.9, 13–14, 37).

## Employer survey findings

- 93% of electronic reach was to SMEs; 89% to non-Chamber members (p.8).
- 1,618 active engagements; 634–635 deep-dive interviews since September 2025 (p.8; Annex C p.5, two slightly different totals given in the two sources).
- Deep-dive interviews by business size: Micro 27%, Small 43%, Medium 23%, Large 7% (Annex C p.9).
- Deep-dive interviews by local authority: Liverpool 38%, Wirral 14%, Halton 16%, Sefton 12%, St Helens 10%, Knowsley 10% (Annex C p.10).
- Vacancy analysis (Annex C p.15–16): 43,555 adverts for business/media/public service professionals and 23,277 for corporate managers/directors (PBS); 41,227 adverts for science/research/engineering/technology professionals and 19,992 for engineering associate professionals (manufacturing); 41,000+ adverts for engineering/technical occupations (construction); 24,841 adverts for health professionals and 16,647 for health/social care associate professionals (health); 9,065 adverts for culture/media/sports occupations (creative).
- DfE Employer Skills Survey 2024 (cited p.42): weak literacy/reading skills contribute to 30% of skill-shortage vacancies nationally. Institute of Student Employers (2025, cited p.42): concern about verbal communication in new entrants rose to 22% (up from 17% in 2024, 7% in 2023).
- Economic inactivity: ~25% in LCR vs ~21% nationally (Annex C p.19; progress report p.8 gives the same 25% figure).

## Discrepancies with the existing app content

1. **`src/data/lsip.ts` priority numbering and names are accurate and match the 2026 LSIP almost exactly** (PBS, Advanced Manufacturing & Clean Energy, Construction, Health/Life Science/Care, Visitor Economy, Creative), including the cross-cutting AI/digital framing. No correction needed to the overall structure.
2. **Defence and maritime**: the existing app content doesn't mention these explicitly, but the 2026 LSIP is explicit that "Defence and maritime are not identified as standalone LSIP priority sectors" — they are folded into Advanced Manufacturing/Clean Energy, Digital & AI, Construction and PBS (p.15). Worth reflecting if defence/maritime roles are ever added, to avoid inventing a standalone sector.
3. **2023 vs 2026 priority sectors changed materially.** The 2023 LSIP's priority sectors were Construction, Manufacturing, **Logistics**, Professional & Business Services and Visitor Economy/Hospitality, with Health & Care explicitly flagged as a "future sector for further analysis" rather than a current priority (2023 LSIP pp.94–199). By 2026, Logistics has been dropped as a standalone priority (folded into manufacturing/maritime framing) and Health, Life Science & Care has been promoted to a full priority. This is a genuine, documented shift in the evidence base, not an error — the 2026 LSIP wins per the brief.
4. **Productivity/GVA figures differ slightly between sources**: the 2026 LSIP main body says GVA per resident is £27,300 (76% of the UK level, p.16), while the 2025 progress report says £25,000 GVA per person (73% of the UK level, p.8). Both are given as authoritative in their own documents; we cite the 2026 figure as primary since it is the newer, in-scope document, and note the progress report's slightly different figure as background context only.
5. **Deep-dive interview count is inconsistent within the same 2026 LSIP**: the main body says 635 (p.8, unlabelled reference) while Annex C's summary table says 634 (Annex C p.5). Both cited; treated as a rounding/typo discrepancy in the source, not corrected by us.
6. **`src/data/lsip-sectors.ts` roles are mostly still valid** but several are not directly evidenced in the 2026 LSIP text (e.g. Architectural technologist, Urban planner, Sustainable tourism officer, Heritage/museum interpreter, Nutrition/wellbeing practitioner, Public health analyst, Clinical trials coordinator). These were dropped from `roles.csv` in favour of roles the 2026 LSIP and Annex A explicitly evidence (e.g. electrician/building services engineer, care worker progression roles, airport ground operations, freelance creative practitioner). This is a refresh, not a claim that the dropped roles are wrong — they may be worth keeping in the app's editorial/quest content even though the LSIP itself doesn't name them as gaps.
7. **AI Task Force footnote is broken in the source PDF**: footnote 14 for "AI for Good" (p.23) reads "????" in the extracted text — apparently a broken hyperlink reference in the original document, not an extraction error (the surrounding text and footnote 15, the AI Taskforce link, extracted cleanly). Flagged as `VERIFY:` in case a working source is needed later.

### Judgement calls and VERIFY flags

1. **Programming's D score was raised from an initial draft of 2 to 3.** A first pass treated all
   `programming` evidence as occupation-table-only (cyber-security-style, capped at 2). On closer
   reading, the Advanced Manufacturing section itself quotes an employer saying welders "will need
   stronger skills in programming, operating and troubleshooting robotic systems" (p.26) — that is a
   stated gap in a priority's own narrative, not just an Annex A occupation row, so it clears the bar
   for D=3 (one priority sector) rather than D=2 (supporting mention). `programming`'s low weight in
   Creative Industries (W=1) stays at occupation-table strength, since Creative's own narrative never
   states a programming-specific shortage, only "fusion skills" and AI-tooling gaps more broadly.
2. **Distinguishing "cross-cutting" from "common but not declared cross-cutting."** Several skills
   (`leadership`, `law-ethics`, `writing`, `speaking`, `self-management`, `problem-solving`,
   `critical-thinking`, `sustainability`, `data-analysis`) were scored D=4 via the cross-cutting route
   because the LSIP explicitly frames them as spanning "all sectors" through one of Changes 1–6 or the
   AI/Digital priority itself — not merely because they happened to recur. Skills that recur across
   sectors only through separate, sector-specific paragraphs (`engineering`, `practical-making`,
   `commercial`) were scored via the "2+ priority sectors" route instead, which reaches the same D=4
   ceiling by a different, equally valid path.
3. **Only `digital-ai` reaches D=5.** This matches the one worked anchor the coordinator supplied, and
   reflects a genuine feature of the source: per methodology §9 (Known limitations #1), LCR gives almost

   claim about digital/AI. No other skill has an equivalent LCR-specific quantified statement (the
   ESS/ISE percentages on p.42 are national figures quoted inside the LSIP and were correctly excluded

4. **`languages` (D=0) has no page citation**, since the whole point is that no LCR document mentions
   foreign languages at all; the `source` cell cites the main LSIP URL with no page, covering all five
   documents reviewed for this region.
5. **Two gaps in `gaps.csv` are about systemic/SME barriers rather than a single named skill**
   ("SMEs struggle to release staff for training…", "A fragmented, freelance-heavy workforce…").
   These were tagged with the closest related skills (`leadership`/`commercial` and
   `self-management`/`commercial` respectively) rather than left thin, since the schema requires at
